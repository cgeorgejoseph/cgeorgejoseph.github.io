Where does ethical reasoning about health-care AI actually happen in public?

Where health-care AI ethics becomes public: documentation as ethics infrastructure in open-source repositories

George Joseph · Digital Policy, Regulation and Governance, ahead of print, 2026

What the paper found

The paper examines how ethical concerns about open-source AI components become publicly sustained and written into documentation. One repository, Fairlearn, repeatedly turns debates into documentation; the other three show more occasional uptake. The paper introduces documentation as ethics infrastructure: the practices through which ethical reasoning becomes durable and open to public inspection.

Key findings

  • Fairlearn repeatedly converts debates about datasets, terminology, intended use and metric interpretation into documentation.
  • AIF360, ClinicalBERT and BioBERT show more episodic reception of such concerns.
  • The clinical repositories are not silent. Model-validity and intended-use concerns do appear, including sustained discussion in BioBERT, but they are weakly classified and unevenly carried into lasting guidance.
  • The pattern is associated with maintenance capacity, contributor pathways, issue classification and coordination across channels. The design does not separate resourcing from topic or repository type.
  • The translation gap is put forward as a proposition for procurement-side testing, not as an outcome observed in these repositories.

What it means in practice

Health-care AI procurement should distinguish the ethical labels attached to a component from the upstream processes that produced them. The paper offers provisional audit prompts showing where ethical reasoning can be publicly inspected; they are not a validated procurement instrument.

How the study was done

A comparative case study of four GitHub repositories (Fairlearn, AIF360, ClinicalBERT and BioBERT) from January 2020 to January 2025. The corpus is 450 keyword-retained issue threads and 2,136 comments, analysed through reflexive thematic analysis, a transparent marker audit and cross-case pattern matching.

Where it is relevant

Useful for work on AI governance, responsible AI, fairness toolkits, clinical language models, open-source communities and how health-care organisations procure and govern AI components they did not build.
